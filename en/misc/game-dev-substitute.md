## Are the Things We Create Just Substitute Goals?

Sometimes I wonder whether the things we choose to do with our lives are really our own goals — or whether they are substitutes for biological goals that feel too difficult, too expensive, or simply unattainable.

Biology gives us some very basic drives: survival, connection, reproduction, security, status. But what happens when one of these goals becomes inaccessible? Perhaps the mind redirects its motivation toward something else — something that is easier to control and still provides a sense of progress, competence, and reward.

For example:

> “I can't achieve what I really want, but I can make a game.”

Creating a game gives immediate evidence of agency. There is an idea in your head, then code, mechanics, sounds, graphics — and eventually something exists that didn't exist before. You can build a small world and make it behave according to rules you designed yourself.

Maybe that is why creating things can feel so meaningful.

But there is an important distinction here. Not every creative goal has to be a substitute for a biological one. Humans seem to have evolved not only to survive and reproduce, but also to explore, play, learn, imagine, and create. Once a brain becomes capable of imagining things that don't exist, it can start pursuing goals that are several steps removed from basic biology.

So perhaps making small games isn't necessarily a replacement for something more “important.”

Maybe it is simply one of the things a particular mind naturally wants to do.

There is an interesting thought experiment here:

**If all my basic biological and social needs were already satisfied, would I still want to make games?**

If the answer is yes — perhaps even more strongly than before — then making games probably isn't merely an escape or substitute. It may be a genuine expression of what that mind finds rewarding.

And even if it is partly a substitute, that doesn't automatically make it meaningless. Humans constantly transform their basic drives into complicated, abstract activities. We don't just seek food; we create cuisine. We don't just seek shelter; we create architecture. We don't just seek connection; we create music, stories, communities, and art.

Perhaps games belong to the same category.

Maybe the question isn't whether a goal is “biological enough.”

Maybe the better question is:

**Does this activity make me feel more alive, more capable, and more like myself — or does it merely help me avoid something I don't want to face?**

The difference between those two can be surprisingly important.
